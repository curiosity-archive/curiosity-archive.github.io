At Crystal Forest in northeastern Arizona, a fallen trunk lies across a pale slope. Its bark-colored sides and blunt ends make it look like timber left behind after a day’s work. The pieces still line up, even where a few have tipped downhill. Look at the broken ends, though, and the resemblance falters. They catch the light like stone because that is what they are, mostly quartz, still carrying the shape of a tree that grew in the Late Triassic. The flat ends are natural fractures. By the time the trunk broke, it had acquired the brittleness of the minerals that filled it. [[1]][wood]

{{< figure src="images/crystal-forest.jpg" alt="A naturally fractured petrified trunk in the pale badlands of Crystal Forest at dawn" class="ma0" >}}

*The sections of this trunk still trace its original length. Photograph by Jacob Holgerson, National Park Service, U.S. public domain. [NPS photo record][crystal-photo]*

That fallen trunk records several different landscapes. It grew in a warm, low-latitude region crossed by rivers. It spent part of its history underground, where water seeped through sediment and left minerals in its cells. And it came back into daylight on an elevated plateau, after erosion stripped away the beds that had sheltered it. The first chapter is easiest to miss because the place where the story began has moved.

## Before there was an Arizona desert

Around 220 million years ago, the ground that would become northeastern Arizona lay much nearer the equator, on the western side of the supercontinent Pangaea. The familiar outlines of North America and its neighboring oceans had yet to form. The future Painted Desert was part of a broad lowland crossed by rivers. Conifers and other seed plants grew there, with ferns and horsetails near the water. The waterways supported fish and long-snouted reptiles called phytosaurs. The basin lay in a monsoon climate, where seasonal shifts in winds brought much of the rain during part of the year. Trees along riverbanks could draw on water flowing from elsewhere in the basin, even as drier ground nearby supported different plants. [[2]][paleo] [[3]][pollen]

{{< figure src="images/late-triassic-pangaea.svg" alt="Reconstruction of Pangaea as a largely connected landmass in the Late Triassic" class="ma0" >}}

*Earth about 220 million years ago. The land that would become Arizona lay on the western side of Pangaea, near the equator. Map by ZomBear, [CC BY 3.0][map-license]. [Map record][pangaea-map] · [Globe from the supplied notes showing the future park site][pangaea-globe]*

Where a river current loses speed, it can drop sand and gravel in the channel. Fine mud can stay suspended long enough to spread across the floodplain, where it settles as floodwater slows. A channel that shifts across its valley can lay sand down over an old floodplain, leaving sandstone over mudstone once those sediments harden. Geologists can still trace some of the old channel deposits through the badlands. At Jasper Forest, a bed of gravelly sandstone rich in petrified wood forms ledges above softer slopes. Deposits like these make up much of the local **Chinle Formation**, the rock unit that holds the famous logs. [[4]][strata] [[5]][martz]

Some trees fell into the water and were carried downstream. Others may have traveled only a short distance. A log can lodge on a bar or catch against another piece of wood, then lie there while the next high water brings sand and mud over it. The park also preserves upright stumps. That variety matters when looking across a slope littered with logs. The trunks that lie together today could have grown apart, and wood concentrated in an ancient channel can tell us as much about where a river dropped its cargo as about where a forest stood. [[1]][wood] [[5]][martz]

Burial also changed what could happen to a trunk. On the surface, fungi and other organisms break down wood, and an exposed trunk can split, fragment, and be scattered. Beneath wet sediment, limited oxygen could slow decomposition long enough for mineral-bearing water to reach the wood while much of its structure was still intact. A river could therefore bring a tree to the place where it would be preserved and furnish the sediment that gave preservation a chance. [[1]][wood] [[6]][mustoe]

## What the water left behind

{{< figure src="images/petrification-stages.png" alt="Three cutaways show flood sediment burying a trunk, groundwater reaching it underground, and erosion exposing the mineralized log" class="ma0" >}}

*Sediment buries the log, silica-bearing groundwater moves through it, and erosion eventually exposes it. The blue arrows represent groundwater flow. AI-generated illustration based on the fossilization process described by the National Park Service and Mustoe. [[1]][wood] [[6]][mustoe]*

A buried tree still had the internal plumbing it used in life. In a conifer, long, narrow cells conduct water and help support the trunk. After death, openings within and between cells gave groundwater routes into the wood. In the Chinle sediments, volcanic ash provided a source of dissolved silica. As water reacted with the ash and circulated through the buried sediments, it could carry silica into the trunk and leave it behind as a solid. [[1]][wood] [[6]][mustoe]

Two processes could proceed in the same piece of wood. Minerals could fill spaces, including the hollow interiors of cells. That filling is called **permineralization**. At the same time, the material of the cell walls could break down as silica took its place, a process called **replacement**. If silica accumulated while the tissues still held their shape, the mineralized wood could preserve the outlines of individual cells. Where decay had already opened a wider cavity, crystals could grow into that space. A cavity full of crystals and a patch of finely preserved grain can therefore record different stages of change within one trunk. [[1]][wood] [[6]][mustoe] [[7]][replacement]

{{< figure src="images/petrified-wood.jpg" alt="Close-up of yellow, purple, reddish and blue-gray areas in petrified wood" class="ma0" >}}

*Color varies over the surface of a piece of petrified wood at Petrified Forest National Park. Photograph by the National Park Service, U.S. public domain. [NPS photo record][wood-photo]*

The colors tell a partly separate story. Pure quartz is colorless. Small amounts of other substances can give the fossil its reds, yellows, and darker patches. Iron can produce different hues depending on its concentration and chemical state. A stripe may follow the original anatomy, but color can also record where minerals entered or accumulated later. In a broken end, the pattern of the tree and the history of the groundwater can occupy the same surface. [[1]][wood] [[6]][mustoe]

How long did it take? The age of a Chinle bed tells us roughly when its wood was buried. Geologists can date tiny crystals of zircon associated with volcanic material by measuring the products of uranium decay. Some of those grains reached the river soon after an eruption, while others were washed out of older rocks and deposited again. That makes the youngest reliable grains a way to constrain when a bed could have formed. The zircon clock dates a volcanic crystal. The conversion of a buried trunk had its own pace. Silica can accumulate in successive episodes, and studies of fossil wood show that the quartz in a log can form through several pathways. Two trunks that lived at about the same time could have had different underground histories. [[6]][mustoe] [[8]][zircon]

## What different fossils preserve

Petrified wood is a **body fossil**. It preserves part of the organism itself, in this case the three-dimensional structure of a tree trunk. Under a microscope, that structure can include the shapes and arrangement of individual cells, even where little of the original wood survives. A **trace fossil** records something an organism did while alive. A footprint can show how an animal placed its feet, and a burrow can show where it moved or sheltered. The first kind of fossil can help identify what lived there. The second can reveal how it behaved. [[6]][mustoe] [[7]][replacement] [[18]][fossil-types]

A tree can leave several kinds of body fossil. A leaf pressed into fine mud can leave an **impression** of its outline and veins after the tissue decays. A flattened leaf can also retain a thin film of carbon-rich material, a kind of **compression**. If a shell or other body part leaves a hollow in the sediment, that hollow is a **mold**. Sediment or minerals can fill it to form a **cast** that reproduces its shape. In some fossils original tissue survives, as when tree resin hardens into amber around an insect. Each route preserves a different part of the evidence. [[18]][fossil-types] [[19]][compressions] [[20]][molds] [[21]][preserved]

{{< figure src="images/fossil-sycamore-leaf.jpg" alt="A dark, flattened sycamore leaf fossil with branching veins visible on a pale slab" class="ma0" >}}

*This sycamore leaf from Oregon’s John Day Fossil Beds is preserved as a compression. Its outline and branching veins are visible on the slab. National Park Service photograph, U.S. public domain. [Specimen and photo credit][compressions]*

In the Chinle, fossil leaves and spores complement the trunks. A trunk’s internal structure tells a paleobotanist about one tree. Leaves and spores can help recover parts of the surrounding vegetation, including plants that left no large pieces of wood. The smaller fossils also give researchers a way to follow changes in the plant communities through successive beds. [[3]][pollen] [[22]][park-plants]

## The layers that went missing

The Triassic rivers themselves changed before their sediments were fully buried. Tiny spores and pollen grains preserved in the Chinle reveal shifts in the kinds of plants growing around the basin. Some of those shifts are consistent with drier conditions during parts of the Late Triassic, although plants also respond to disturbance and to changes in where streams and wetlands lie. Fossil soils add evidence at a different scale. In some Chinle beds, greenish and bluish colors are associated with soils that stayed waterlogged, while reddish layers preserve iron minerals that oxidized when oxygen could reach them. Those colors can record differences in local drainage. Pollen gives a broader view of how vegetation changed over time. [[3]][pollen] [[4]][strata]

For what came later, look north from Nizhoni Point. Red and pale Chinle beds lie beneath a dark cap of much younger volcanic rock associated with the Bidahochi Formation. The boundary between them is an **unconformity**, a surface that represents time missing from the local sequence because sediment either never accumulated there or was subsequently removed. At this contact the gap spans roughly 190 million years. It leaves no continuous record at the overlook of how the Triassic basin became a high, semiarid landscape. [[4]][strata]

{{< figure src="images/nizhoni-point.jpg" alt="Dark volcanic rock capping red and pale Chinle slopes north of Nizhoni Point" class="ma0" >}}

*At Nizhoni Point, younger Bidahochi basalt caps the much older Chinle beds. Photograph by the National Park Service, U.S. public domain. [NPS geology and photo credit][strata]*

Elsewhere on the Colorado Plateau, rocks preserve some of those intervening scenes. At Navajo National Monument, sweeping bands in the Navajo Sandstone follow the slopes of Jurassic dunes. A much later inland sea reached parts of the region during the Cretaceous. The Triassic floodplain, Jurassic dune fields, and Cretaceous marine deposits belong to a region whose shorelines, drainage, and climate were repeatedly remade. [[9]][dunes] [[10]][uplift]

Then the ground rose. Starting in the Late Cretaceous, during the mountain-building episode known as the Laramide orogeny, parts of the region began gaining elevation. Further changes followed, at different times across the plateau. Much of its sedimentary rock remained broadly layered even as the land rose, which helps explain the combination of high country, flat-topped mesas, and deep valleys. Regional drainage reorganized, and streams cut into the raised terrain. The plateau’s rise and its dissection were related processes unfolding over a long interval. [[10]][uplift]

Today the park sits far from its Triassic latitude, within a continent with different coastlines and mountain ranges. The modern Southwest gets moisture from cool-season Pacific storms and a summer monsoon. Mountain ranges and elevation affect where that moisture falls. Moist air can cool as it rises over a range, bringing rain or snow to the side facing the wind and leaving drier conditions on the far side. This effect is called a **rain shadow**. In the park, winter can bring snow to the badlands, while summer thunderstorms supply much of the year’s rain. Water that runs off bare slopes picks up fine sediment and carries it downhill. [[4]][strata] [[11]][climate] [[12]][weather] [[23]][rain-shadow]

{{< figure src="images/rain-shadow.svg" alt="A schematic shows moist air rising and releasing rain on the Pacific-facing mountain slope, then descending toward the inland Southwest" class="ma0" >}}

*An idealized rain shadow shows one way mountains redistribute precipitation. Winds and sources of moisture in the Southwest vary by season. ChatGPT-generated diagram from the supplied notes.*

Erosion brings a log into view one edge at a time. The relatively soft mudstone wears away around the hard fossil. A slope may release a piece and let it tumble downhill, so a fossil on the surface may have moved again since it was first buried. The nearly flat breaks at Crystal Forest reflect the brittleness the wood acquired underground. The National Park Service attributes the fracturing of many buried logs to stress during the plateau’s uplift. Continued erosion makes those breaks visible and can scatter the pieces. The same processes that expose the fossils gradually take them apart. [[1]][wood] [[4]][strata]

## Farther west, a river cuts a canyon

The Grand Canyon shows what sustained erosion can do when a major river gains a route across the Colorado Plateau. The pale Kaibab Formation at the South Rim dates to about 270 million years ago, before the Chinle trees grew. Some of the rocks near the bottom are more than 1.7 billion years old. Both were in place long before the river cut the gorge. The canyon walls bring rocks from widely separated periods of Earth’s history into view, with gaps where layers were eroded or never deposited. [[13]][ages] [[14]][canyon]

{{< figure src="images/grand-canyon.jpg" alt="Layered walls and branching ravines of the Grand Canyon from Moran Point on the South Rim" class="ma0" >}}

*From Moran Point, tributary ravines cut into the layered walls of the Grand Canyon. Photograph by the National Park Service, U.S. public domain. [NPS photo record][canyon-photo]*

Evidence from different reaches suggests that the modern canyon took shape beginning about five to six million years ago, as the Colorado River linked some older valleys and cut new passages between them. Once the river had a connected route toward a lower outlet, flowing water and the sand and gravel it carried could scour the bed. Tributaries cut into the sides, weaker layers eroded back from more resistant cliffs, and blocks fell from slopes undercut by erosion. The river lowered the floor while those processes widened the gorge. [[14]][canyon] [[15]][incision]

There is a clue to how the route came together back in northeastern Arizona. The same Bidahochi Formation that lies above the Chinle in the Painted Desert also records lakes in a much younger basin east of Flagstaff. In a 2026 study, researchers compared the ages and sources of mineral grains in the basin’s lake beds with those in early Colorado River deposits. They found that sediment from the ancestral Colorado–Green River system had reached the basin by about 6.6 million years ago. Their evidence supports an idea geologists have debated for decades. A lake fed by that river system could have risen high enough to spill westward, helping establish the river’s course through the canyon. [[4]][strata] [[16]][spillover]

A second team traced a similar sequence using a different mineral, a kind of feldspar called sanidine, and compared it with evidence from the ancestry of river fish. Both studies place the ancestral river in the Bidahochi Basin before it had a continuous route to the sea. The second team gives more weight to tectonic changes that raised some areas and lowered others, shifting the slopes that governed where rivers could flow. The mineral grains establish where sediment went. Geologists are still working out what opened the way. [[16]][spillover] [[17]][tectonics]

At Crystal Forest, the evidence fits in the end of a log. The mineralized grain survives because a river buried the tree and water reached it underground. At the Grand Canyon, the evidence stands in the walls because a later river carried rock away. Between the two places, the Bidahochi beds preserve a moment when a river was still finding its way across northern Arizona. [[1]][wood] [[15]][incision] [[16]][spillover]
